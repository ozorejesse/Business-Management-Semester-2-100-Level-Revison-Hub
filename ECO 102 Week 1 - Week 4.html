1. Week 1: The Circular Flow Diagram (Two-Sector Economy)

Provides a primary model explaining how income is generated, distributed, and redistributed within a macroeconomy across time.

Key Economic Agents

  • Households: Aggregated individual consumer units that own all factors of production (land, labour, capital, entrepreneurship) and consume finished goods/services to maximize utility.
  • Business Units (Firms): Entities responsible for harnessing factor inputs from households to produce finished goods and services.

Core Assumptions of the Basic Model

  • 1. Households do not invest: They consume goods and services exclusively for utility.
  • 2. Business units do not consume: They harness inputs to produce finished output.
  • 3. Households own factor inputs: Inputs are supplied to firms at a market price.
  • 4. Closed System (No Leakages or Injections): Money generated remains entirely within the flow cycle; no government ($T, G$), financial markets ($S, I$), or foreign sector ($M, X$).

Real Flow vs. Money Flow

  • Real Flow (Outer Loop): Physical movement of factors of production from Households to Firms, and finished Goods & Services from Firms to Households.
  • Money Flow (Inner Loop): Monetary exchange of Factor Payments (wages, rent, interest, profit) from Firms to Households, and Consumption Expenditure from Households to Firms.
Commodity Market: Goods & Services $\longleftrightarrow$ Consumption Spending
Factor Market: Factors of Production $\longleftrightarrow$ Factor Income (Wages, Rent, Interest, Profits)

2. Week 2: Circular Money Flow with Saving and Investment

Expands the two-sector model by incorporating the financial sector as an intermediary between household savings and firm investments.

Leakages vs. Injections

  • Leakages (Withdrawals): Portions of income diverted away from the immediate consumption stream. Household Saving ($S$) is the primary leakage in this expanded model.
  • Injections: Additions of funds into the circular flow stream. Business Investment ($I$) represents the primary injection.
  • Financial Intermediaries: Banks and financial institutions channel saved funds from households into loanable funds for business capital investment.

Equilibrium & The Savings-Investment Identity

  • For the circular flow to maintain constancy (equilibrium), aggregate leakages must equal aggregate injections: $$S = I$$
  • If $S > I$: Excess savings cause aggregate expenditure to fall below aggregate output. Unsold inventory rises, causing firms to cut production, income, and employment.
  • If $S < I$: Excess investment boosts aggregate demand beyond current production capacity, putting upward pressure on interest rates, output, and income.
Income Allocation: $Y = C + S$
Aggregate Expenditure: $E = C + I$
Equilibrium Condition: $Y = E \implies C + S = C + I \implies S = I$

3. Week 3: Average and Marginal Propensity to Consume (APC & MPC)

Explores the relationship between consumption expenditure ($C$) and income ($Y$), deriving key propensities based on Keynesian macroeconomic principles.

Average Propensity to Consume (APC)

  • The proportion of total disposable income allocated towards consumption at a given income level: $$APC = \frac{C}{Y}$$
  • Key Feature: Ranges between $0$ and $1$ (or $>1$ when dissaving occurs). $APC$ falls as income rises because consumption increases at a slower rate than income.

Marginal Propensity to Consume (MPC)

  • The ratio of the change in consumption ($\Delta C$) resulting from a unit change in income ($\Delta Y$): $$MPC = \frac{\Delta C}{\Delta Y}$$
  • Reflects marginal decision-making behavior ($0 < MPC < 1$). For instance, an $MPC = 0.8$ means $80\%$ of additional income is spent, and $20\%$ is saved.
Consumption Identity: $Y = C + S \implies 1 = \frac{C}{Y} + \frac{S}{Y}$
Marginal Identity: $\Delta Y = \Delta C + \Delta S \implies 1 = \frac{\Delta C}{\Delta Y} + \frac{\Delta S}{\Delta Y}$

4. Week 4: Average and Marginal Propensity to Save (APS & MPS)

Examines the saving behavior of households as the counterpart to consumption behavior.

Average Propensity to Save (APS)

  • The proportion of aggregate disposable income saved: $$APS = \frac{S}{Y}$$
  • Because $Y = C + S$, dividing by $Y$ gives $APC + APS = 1 \implies APS = 1 - APC$.
  • $APS$ can be negative at low income levels where consumption exceeds income ($C > Y$, i.e., dissaving).

Marginal Propensity to Save (MPS)

  • The fraction of an incremental change in income that is saved: $$MPS = \frac{\Delta S}{\Delta Y}$$
  • Because $\Delta Y = \Delta C + \Delta S$, dividing by $\Delta Y$ yields: $$MPC + MPS = 1 \implies MPS = 1 - MPC$$
Fundamental Propensity Identities:
$APC + APS = 1$
$MPC + MPS = 1$

5. Week 4: The Savings Function & Derivation

Derives the explicit mathematical savings function from the linear Keynesian consumption function $C = a + bY$.

Derivation Steps

  • Given $Y = C + S \implies S = Y - C$.
  • Substitute $C = a + bY$: $$S = Y - (a + bY) = -a + Y - bY = -a + (1 - b)Y$$
  • Where $-a$ is autonomous dissaving (negative intercept) and $(1 - b) = MPS$.

Key Characteristics of the Savings Function Graph

  • Negative Intercept ($-a$): Represents dissavings funded by borrowing or drawing down past savings when income $Y = 0$.
  • Break-Even Point ($S = 0$): Point where disposable income equals consumption ($Y = C$). At this point, $APC = 1$ and $APS = 0$.
  • Positive Slope ($MPS > 0$): Savings increase as income increases.
Keynesian Consumption: $C = a + bY \quad (a > 0, 0 < b < 1)$
Keynesian Savings: $S = -a + (1 - b)Y$
Break-Even Condition: $S = 0 \implies Y = C$
