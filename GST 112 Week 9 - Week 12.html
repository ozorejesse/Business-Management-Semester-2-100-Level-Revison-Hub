WEEK 9

Reorientation Strategies

Learning focus: define reorientation strategies, examine Nigerian government initiatives and assess the extent to which they achieved their objectives.

1. Meaning and purpose of reorientation

Reorientation strategies are efforts by government authorities, institutions or organised programmes to reshape or redirect people's attitudes, beliefs and behaviour towards agreed ideals. They seek to encourage positive social change through public awareness campaigns, education, skills development, counselling and community outreach.

In Nigeria, these initiatives have commonly promoted patriotism, national unity, discipline, self-reliance, responsible citizenship and development. Their success depends not only on public campaigns but also on credible leadership, adequate planning, resources and consistent implementation.

2. National Orientation Agency (NOA)

The lecture identifies the NOA as the successor to the Directorate for Social Mobilisation (MAMSER). It was established by Decree 100 of 1993. Its role is to create and sustain public awareness, understanding and support for government policies, programmes and initiatives, while encouraging positive values, patriotism, national unity and development.

Examples of NOA activities in the lecture include attitudinal reorientation for tertiary students; implementation of a National Ethics and Integrity Policy in secondary schools; campaigns against open defecation; inter-faith peaceful coexistence under “Building a People of Peace”; sensitisation on the Freedom of Information Act, COVID-19 and routine immunisation; and “Change Begins With Me”, which encouraged patronage of made-in-Nigeria products and services.

3. Operation Feed the Nation (OFN)

OFN was an agricultural initiative launched in 1976 by the military government of General Olusegun Obasanjo. It aimed to improve food security and reduce dependence on imported food. Urban residents were encouraged to cultivate available plots, while farmers were to receive awareness, training and agricultural inputs such as improved seeds, fertilisers and farm implements. Sustainable farming and conservation were also emphasised.

The lecture links OFN with programmes including Agricultural Development Projects (1976), Agricultural Credit Guarantee Scheme (1977), River Basin Development Programme (1978), Rural Banking System (1978), Green Revolution (1980), and the Directorate for Food, Roads and Rural Infrastructure (1986). The material notes implementation problems: promised resources such as seedlings, insecticides and tractors were not adequately supplied; activities were poorly performed; some farmers were sceptical of new methods; administrative officials did not perform as expected; and the manpower needed to expand farming did not materialise. Although OFN did not fully meet its objectives, it raised awareness of agriculture and influenced later agricultural programmes.

4. Austerity measures

Austerity measures are restrictive or frugal economic policies used by governments to manage public debt, budget deficits or economic hardship. The lecture discusses austerity after the oil boom of the 1970s, during Buhari's military regime, under Babangida's Structural Adjustment Programme (SAP), and from 2015 in response to lower oil prices and output.

Examples listed include fiscal consolidation, reducing recurrent expenditure, subsidy reforms, diversifying revenue, debt management and rationalising subsidies and social programmes. The lecture groups austerity approaches into: raising taxes to fund spending; raising revenue while cutting spending; and lowering taxes while also lowering spending. It criticises austerity in Nigeria for repeatedly shifting much of the burden to ordinary people without adequately addressing government waste and spending.

5. War Against Indiscipline (WAI)

WAI was a mass mobilisation programme organised by Buhari's military government to correct social maladjustments and promote discipline, moral values and national pride. Its messages included punctuality, orderly queuing, environmental cleanliness and obedience to traffic regulations. It combined public sensitisation, penalties and public works intended to improve infrastructure and cleanliness.

The lecture describes the campaign as achieving visible discipline, partly through the forceful actions of the WAI Brigade. However, the same high-handedness that helped enforce compliance attracted criticism. The programme ended in September 1985. This illustrates the tension between promoting public order and respecting citizens' rights.

6. MAMSER

Mass Mobilisation for Self-Reliance, Social Justice and Economic Recovery (MAMSER) was launched in 1987 following recommendations of the Political Bureau headed by Samuel Cookey. It aimed to mobilise and empower citizens to participate in nation-building, promote self-reliance, advance social justice and support economic recovery. The lecture notes its role in the emergence of cooperative societies in Nigerian workplaces. It was later phased out, with its functions absorbed into other government agencies and initiatives. Its lasting effectiveness remains open to assessment.

7. Why reorientation programmes struggle

The lecture's conclusion points to corruption, lack of political will, poor leadership, inadequate exemplary leadership and improper planning as reasons reorientation efforts have often failed. A programme cannot sustainably change public behaviour if public officials contradict the values they promote, if resources are not delivered, or if enforcement is arbitrary. The material calls for renewed emphasis on trust, legitimate work and moral conscience.

WEEK 10

Police Brutality, #EndSARS Crisis, the 2023 Election and Youth Political Awakening

Learning focus: connect police brutality with youth reawakening, describe #EndSARS and examine its relationship to the 2023 elections.

1. Youth and Nigeria's political future

The lecture describes Nigerian youth as roughly 60% of the population and therefore highly influential to the country's future. It warns that a large youth population that is not effectively engaged can become a source of social upheaval. The 2020 protests and the 2023 elections highlighted young people's dissatisfaction with existing governance and the importance of youth participation.

2. Understanding police brutality

Police brutality includes excessive use of force, extrajudicial killings, harassment and abuse of authority by law-enforcement agencies. The lecture presents these as longstanding problems in Nigeria. The #EndSARS movement brought international attention to police misconduct and demanded accountability for the now-disbanded Special Anti-Robbery Squad (SARS).

The material cites Moses Ochonu's argument that the Nigerian Police had not been decolonised: colonial policing emphasised suppression of revolts rather than public protection, with brute force as a central operating philosophy. It also presents Abimbola Oyarinu's explanation that SARS emerged amid national turmoil, including economic decline after the oil boom, a depreciating naira, the Civil War, the Structural Adjustment Programme and poor leadership, which contributed to insecurity and violent crime.

The lecture reports that The Cable identified 91 deaths caused by police actions between March 2019 and February 2020. It links impunity to courts and a justice system perceived as slow, inefficient, incompetent or insufficiently respected to hold officers accountable.

3. The #EndSARS movement

#EndSARS was a decentralised social movement and series of mass protests against police brutality. The agitation called for SARS to be disbanded because the unit was associated with unlawful activities, particularly against young people. The hashtag began on Twitter in 2017 and developed into a major crisis in October 2020. The lecture identifies as an immediate trigger a video-recorded incident in which a police officer targeted an unarmed civilian.

In October 2020, thousands of young Nigerians protested against police brutality and extrajudicial killings. The lecture describes a three-week period of demonstrations, violence, attacks on two correctional facilities in Edo State, vandalism and looting. It also recounts the army's use of gunfire against protesters at the Lekki toll gate on 20 October 2020, with deaths and injuries reported.

4. Social media and mobilisation

Social media helped the movement spread information, coordinate collective action and attract local and international attention. Protesters used e-flyers to circulate locations and times, and Twitter was particularly important. The lecture compares this mobilisation dynamic with the Arab Spring and notes that the protests spread nationwide, though they were less prominent in northern Nigeria.

5. Government response and post-EndSARS concerns

The government's initial attempts to stop the protests aggravated tensions. SARS was eventually dissolved and police reform was promised, but the lecture says discernible progress was limited. It cites Global Rights' record of 164 extrajudicial killings between January and September 2021, one year after the crisis. It also cites Amnesty International records for the South-East: 52 unlawful killings and 62 cases of arbitrary arrest, ill-treatment and torture over four months from January 2021. These figures are presented in the lecture to show the continuing need for systemic police reform.

6. The 2023 elections

The lecture connects the end of the 2020 protests with young people's decision to channel anger and demands into electoral participation. It states that around 76% of newly registered voters were young people and that 40% of those young registrants were students. It also notes that 87.2 million of 93.4 million registered voters collected their Permanent Voter Cards, while national turnout was 29%, described in the material as the lowest in Nigeria's six decades of independence.

7. Youth political awakening

The movement helped stimulate political discussion, demands for accountability and active participation. The lecture identifies youth-led advocacy groups, voter-registration drives and calls for greater representation of young people in political office as expressions of this awakening. Social media became an important tool for amplifying youth voices and organising collective action. The central connection is that protest over police abuse developed into broader demands for good governance, transparency and an end to systemic corruption.

WEEK 11

Makers of Nigeria

Learning focus: identify Nigerians in politics, the economy and creative industries; examine their contributions and motivations.

1. Why study the makers of Nigeria?

The lecture examines individuals from different walks of life whose work shaped Nigeria's political development, national identity, public life and creative culture. Their contributions include nationalist organisation, political leadership, education, public service, cultural expression and challenging colonial or postcolonial power. Studying them helps connect individual action to wider national development.

2. Herbert Macaulay (1864–1946)

Herbert Macaulay is described as the father of Nigerian nationalism and one of the figures who laid the foundation of modern Nigeria. During the 1920s, he opposed colonial policies involving water rates, land issues and railway finances. In 1923, he founded the Nigerian National Democratic Party (NNDP), identified in the lecture as Nigeria's first political party. Its members became the first to sit in the Legislative Council, and the party remained influential until the late 1930s. It later joined forces with the Nigerian Youth Movement to form the National Council of Nigeria and the Cameroons (NCNC), a nationalist organisation advocating independence.

3. Obafemi Awolowo (1909–1987)

Awolowo was the first Premier of the Western Region from 1954 to 1960 and an active nationalist in the struggle for independence. The lecture presents him as a major political figure whose leadership and ideas contributed to the development of regional government and Nigeria's political history. In studying his contribution, connect his nationalist activity with his role in regional administration and public policy.

4. Nnamdi Azikiwe

The lecture notes that Azikiwe inspired the Zikist movement, described as a radical, revolutionary and multi-ethnic youth body. It championed the struggle against imperialism and the belief that Nigerians and Africans should manage their own affairs. This illustrates the role of political ideas and youth mobilisation in anti-colonial nationalism.

5. Fela Anikulapo-Kuti (1938–1997)

Fela used music and lyrics as forms of protest, resistance and commentary on political events. As he became prominent during the 1970s, he changed the relationship between music as an art and Nigerian socio-political discourse. The lecture credits him as the creator of Afrobeat in the early 1970s. His example shows how creative expression can challenge authority, communicate social criticism and influence public debate beyond formal politics.

6. Understanding their different contributions

The people discussed in the lecture should not be treated as if they all contributed in the same way. Macaulay's role is associated with early party politics and organised nationalism; Awolowo with nationalist leadership and regional governance; Azikiwe with nationalist ideas and youth mobilisation; and Fela with music as political expression and cultural influence. Together, these examples show that nation-building can take place through political organisation, public leadership, ideas, culture and civic resistance.

WEEK 12

Social Vices: Cultism, Kidnapping and Armed Robbery

Learning focus: distinguish crime from deviance, explain theories of deviance and understand selected social vices.

1. Meaning of social vices

Social vices are actions that depart from accepted moral norms. The lecture lists examples such as prostitution, indecent dressing, robbery, cultism, pickpocketing, drug addiction, examination fraud, hooliganism, thuggery, gambling, smoking, premarital sexual activity and rape. The lesson focuses particularly on cultism, kidnapping and armed robbery.

2. Crime and deviance

Crime and deviance are related but not identical. Crime involves violating formal laws and may attract legal punishment. Deviance involves behaviour that violates social norms, expectations or rules. Some conduct may be both criminal and deviant, but not every departure from a social norm is necessarily a crime. The distinction matters because legal rules and social expectations are not the same thing.

3. Theories of deviance

Structural functionalism: the lecture explains that deviance can have a social function by bringing members of society together to define and reaffirm what is acceptable and unacceptable. When a community reacts to behaviour it considers deviant, it can reinforce shared norms and social boundaries.

Social strain typology: this approach relates deviant behaviour to two criteria: a person's motivation or acceptance of culturally approved goals, and the person's belief in or access to legitimate means of attaining those goals. The gap between socially promoted goals and available legitimate opportunities can help explain why some individuals turn to deviant adaptations.

4. Cultism

The lecture treats cultism as a serious social vice and crime because it threatens social harmony and stability. It can cause loss of life, interrupt education and create fear in affected communities. Cult-related activity can also damage the safety and reputation of educational institutions and surrounding communities. Prevention requires attention to the conditions that make recruitment attractive, effective institutional discipline, community awareness and lawful enforcement.

5. Kidnapping

Kidnapping is the unlawful abduction and detention of a person, often for financial gain, ransom or other motives such as revenge. It inflicts trauma on victims and their families, creates fear in society and discourages investment and economic development. Its effects therefore extend beyond the immediate victim: families may face financial pressure, communities may restrict movement and businesses may avoid insecure locations.

6. Armed robbery

Armed robbery is a serious threat to public safety. It creates fear, reduces trust in law enforcement and hampers economic growth. The lecture includes prison-record figures of 11,858 armed robbery cases in 2013 and 10,249 in 2014. These are figures reported in the lecture and should be understood in that source context rather than treated as current crime statistics.

7. Wider consequences and responses

The three featured vices undermine personal security, community confidence, education, investment and social stability. Responses need to combine prevention and accountability: strengthen lawful policing and justice processes, reduce opportunities for recruitment into violent groups, support education and legitimate livelihoods, encourage community reporting, and protect victims. The lecture's emphasis on informed decisions means recognising the harm caused by these behaviours and rejecting participation in them.
