1. Core Communication Skills & Listening Modes

Explores foundational skills in listening, speaking, reading, and writing, distinguishing between active, engaged processing and passive, uncritical reception[cite: 1].

Key Concepts & Breakdown

  • Receptive vs. Expressive Skills: Listening and Reading are receptive skills used to decode meaning; Speaking and Writing are productive/expressive skills used to encode messages[cite: 1].
  • The Communication Loop: Requires a Sender, Message, Encoding process, Transmission Channel, Receiver, Decoding process, and Feedback loop to confirm accuracy[cite: 1].
  • Active Listening: A deliberate cognitive process involving undivided attention, note-taking, critical synthesis, tone analysis, and non-verbal feedback[cite: 1].
  • Passive Listening: Receiving sound without evaluation, questioning, or critical absorption, often leading to partial information retention[cite: 1].
Communication Loop: Sender ➔ Message ➔ Channel ➔ Receiver ➔ Feedback[cite: 1]
Listening Modes: Active (Analytical/Critical/Engaged) vs. Passive (Uncritical/Inattentive)[cite: 1]

2. The 8 Parts of Speech

Covers the essential structural units of English sentences: Nouns, Pronouns, Verbs, Adjectives, Adverbs, Prepositions, Conjunctions, and Interjections[cite: 2].

Key Concepts & Breakdown

  • Nouns & Pronouns: Nouns name people, places, things, or abstract ideas[cite: 2]. Pronouns replace nouns to prevent repetition (e.g., personal, relative, demonstrative)[cite: 2].
  • Verbs: Express actions, states of being, or linking conditions (e.g., action verbs vs. auxiliary/linking verbs)[cite: 2].
  • Modifiers: Adjectives modify or describe nouns/pronouns[cite: 2]. Adverbs modify verbs, adjectives, or other adverbs (often answering how, when, where, why, or to what extent)[cite: 2].
  • Structural Connectors: Prepositions establish spatial, temporal, or logical relationships[cite: 2]. Conjunctions join words, phrases, or clauses (coordinating vs. subordinating)[cite: 2]. Interjections express sudden emotion[cite: 2].
Content-Heavy Core: Noun + Pronoun + Verb + Adjective + Adverb[cite: 2, 4]
Structural Connectors: Preposition + Conjunction + Interjection[cite: 2, 4]

3. Sentence Structures & Functions

Analyzes clause arrangements (Simple, Compound, Complex, Compound-Complex) and expressive purposes (Declarative, Imperative, Interrogative, Exclamatory, Conditional)[cite: 3].

Key Concepts & Breakdown

  • Simple Sentences: Contain one independent clause (one subject and one predicate)[cite: 3].
  • Compound Sentences: Contain two or more independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction (FANBOYS) or a semicolon[cite: 3, 6].
  • Complex Sentences: Contain one independent clause and at least one dependent clause joined by a subordinating conjunction[cite: 3].
  • Compound-Complex Sentences: Contain at least two independent clauses and one or more dependent clauses[cite: 3].
  • Sentence Functions: Declarative (makes a statement), Imperative (gives a command), Interrogative (asks a question), Exclamatory (expresses strong emotion), and Conditional (states a scenario and outcome)[cite: 3].
Complex = 1 Independent Clause + ≥1 Dependent Clause[cite: 3]
Compound-Complex = ≥2 Independent Clauses + ≥1 Dependent Clause[cite: 3]

4. Word Classes: Content vs. Function Words

Distinguishes open-class content words that convey primary semantic meaning from closed-class function words that provide grammatical connections and prosodic structure[cite: 4].

Key Concepts & Breakdown

  • Content Words (Open Class): Include Nouns, Main Verbs, Adjectives, and Adverbs[cite: 4]. New words can be continually added to the language[cite: 4]. They carry primary meaning and receive main vocal stress in speech[cite: 4].
  • Function Words (Closed Class): Include Pronouns, Auxiliary Verbs, Prepositions, Conjunctions, Articles, and Determiners[cite: 4]. They serve purely structural or grammatical roles and are generally unstressed in spoken English[cite: 4].
  • Prosodic Timing: The alternation between stressed content words and unstressed function words gives spoken English its characteristic rhythm[cite: 4].
Content Words (Stressed): Nouns, Main Verbs, Adjectives, Adverbs[cite: 4]
Function Words (Unstressed): Pronouns, Auxiliaries, Prepositions, Conjunctions, Articles[cite: 4]

5. Paragraph Structure & Development

Covers structural components of paragraphs including topic sentences, supporting details, coherence, unity, and transitional phrases[cite: 5].

Key Concepts & Breakdown

  • Topic Sentence: Directs the primary focus and central argument of the entire paragraph[cite: 5].
  • Supporting Details: Provide evidence, statistical data, explanations, and concrete illustrations[cite: 5].
  • Paragraph Unity: Achieved when every single sentence relates directly back to the main topic sentence[cite: 5].
  • Paragraph Coherence: Logical, smooth transition from one sentence to the next using pronouns, parallel structures, and transitional words (e.g., however, furthermore, consequently)[cite: 5].
  • Concluding Sentence: Summarizes the main point or provides a transition to the next paragraph[cite: 5].
Paragraph Anatomy = Topic Sentence + Supporting Details + Concluding Sentence[cite: 5]
Core Attributes = Unity (Single idea focus) + Coherence (Logical sequential flow)[cite: 5]

6. Punctuation Mechanics & Usage

Rules and applications for 13 essential punctuation marks to establish clear pause structure, tone, syntactic relationship, and text boundary[cite: 6].

Key Concepts & Breakdown

  • Terminal Marks: Periods (.), Question Marks (?), and Exclamation Points (!) end distinct sentences[cite: 6].
  • Pauses & Links: Commas (,) separate items in lists or introductory clauses[cite: 6]. Semicolons (;) connect independent clauses without conjunctions[cite: 6]. Colons (:) introduce lists, explanations, or quotes[cite: 6].
  • Enclosure & Separation: Apostrophes (') show possession or contractions[cite: 6]. Quotation marks (" ") enclose direct discourse[cite: 6]. Parentheses ( ) and Brackets [ ] enclose supplemental comments or edits[cite: 6]. Hyphens (-) join compound words while Dashes (—) emphasize pauses[cite: 6].
Punctuation Kit: Period, Comma, Semicolon, Colon, Apostrophe, Quotations, Hyphen, Dash, etc.[cite: 6]
Semicolon Rule: Independent Clause + ; + Independent Clause[cite: 6]
