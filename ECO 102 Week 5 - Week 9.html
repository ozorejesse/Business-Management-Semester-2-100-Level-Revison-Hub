Week 5: Addressing Inflation and Inflationary Trends in Selected Economies

InflationMonetary policyFiscal policyInternational comparison

Learning objectives: Explain ways of addressing inflation; discuss policy interventions; explain why inflation varies across countries; and analyse those variations.

1. Meaning and effects of inflation

Inflation is the rate at which the prices of goods and services increase over time. It is a general rise in prices, not simply the increase in the price of one isolated product. Inflation affects households, firms and the economy as a whole.

  • Purchasing power: When the general price level rises, a given amount of money buys fewer goods and services. If income does not rise at the same pace as prices, the real purchasing power of households falls.
  • Consumer confidence: High and unpredictable inflation makes it harder for consumers to plan spending. Uncertainty about future prices can reduce confidence and make people reluctant to spend.
  • Business profitability: Rising input and operating costs can squeeze business margins, especially when firms cannot pass all cost increases to customers.
  • Investment: Uncertainty about future costs, prices and interest rates can discourage investment.
  • Economic stability: Severe inflation can contribute to instability and social unrest.

The lecture identifies supply-chain disruptions, strong demand for goods and services, and geopolitical events such as the war in Ukraine as factors associated with rising inflation. It also refers to demand-pull and cost-push inflation when explaining why inflation differs between countries.

2. Main ways of addressing inflation

  • Setting an inflation target: A central bank announces a desired inflation rate and aims to keep inflation close to it over the medium term. The lecture notes that some central banks use a target around 2%; this is an illustration, not a universal target for every country.
  • Conducting monetary policy: Central banks influence the money supply, interest rates and economic activity. Adjusting policy rates and conducting open market operations are examples of tools used to manage inflationary pressure.
  • Interest-rate policy: A central bank may raise its policy interest rate to cool an overheating economy. Higher borrowing costs can reduce borrowing, spending and investment, easing demand pressure.
  • Open market operations: Central banks buy or sell government securities. Selling securities withdraws money from the financial system, tends to raise interest rates and can curb inflation. Buying securities has the opposite effect.
  • Quantitative tightening (QT): A central bank reduces the size of its balance sheet, for example by selling assets. This can absorb excess liquidity from the financial system.
  • Forward guidance: The central bank communicates its future policy intentions. Clear communication about its commitment to controlling inflation can influence expectations among the public and financial markets.
  • Coordination with fiscal policy: Monetary policy can be complemented by government taxation and spending decisions. The lecture presents coordination between fiscal and monetary policy as a way to pursue price stability.
  • Inflation forecasting: Central banks analyse economic indicators and inflation expectations to assess future price pressures and make informed policy decisions.
  • Exchange-rate policies: A stronger domestic currency can reduce the domestic cost of imported goods, potentially lowering imported inflation. The lecture notes that exchange-rate policies may be used to influence inflation.
  • Supply-side policies: Improving productivity, removing regulatory bottlenecks and enhancing competition can address root causes of rising production costs and help reduce cost-push inflation.
Remember the direction of open market operations: Selling securities → less money/liquidity in the system → upward pressure on interest rates → reduced inflationary pressure. Buying securities generally works in the opposite direction.

3. Taxation as a fiscal-policy response

The lecture's answer to its mid-lesson question explains three channels through which taxation can help address inflation:

  • Reducing disposable income: Higher personal taxes reduce the disposable income available to households. Lower disposable income can reduce consumer spending and cool excessive demand.
  • Supply-side tax policies: Lower corporate taxes or tax incentives for businesses may improve efficiency and productivity. Stronger productive capacity can help an economy meet demand without generating as much inflationary pressure.
  • Budget surpluses: Higher tax revenue can contribute to a budget surplus. The surplus may be used to pay down debt or fund infrastructure, supporting fiscal discipline and reducing inflationary pressure.

4. Why inflation varies across countries

Inflation occurs in countries around the world, but it does not occur at the same rate or intensity. The lecture groups the explanations into economic conditions, policy choices, exchange rates, supply-and-demand dynamics and demographic factors.

  • Economic conditions: Strong economic growth can increase demand for goods and services and create inflationary pressure.
  • Monetary policy: Differences in interest rates, money-supply growth and other central-bank tools can produce different inflation outcomes.
  • Fiscal policy: Government spending and taxation affect aggregate demand. Excessive government spending may add to inflationary pressure.
  • Exchange rates: A depreciating currency can make imported goods more expensive, particularly in countries that are net importers.
  • Supply shocks: Natural disasters, geopolitical events and production-chain disruptions can reduce supply and push prices upward.
  • Demand changes: Differences in consumer and business spending patterns influence total demand and price levels.
  • Population growth: Different population growth rates can change demand for goods and services.
  • Age structure: The age composition of a population can affect consumption patterns and economic activity.

5. Comparing selected economies: what the lecture charts show

Chart on page 12–13: The lecture compares the GDP-deflator inflation rates of Nigeria, South Africa, the United Kingdom and the United States over 2010–2020. It describes Nigeria as having the highest inflation rate among the four for much of the period, although Nigeria's rate was below South Africa's between 2013 and 2015. The United States had the lowest rate in 2020, while the United Kingdom had the lowest rate for most of 2010–2019.

Important chart detail: The comparison uses the GDP deflator and the period 2010–2020. Do not treat the historical chart as current inflation data.

6. Quick revision points

  • Inflation reduces the purchasing power of money when prices rise.
  • Central banks may use interest rates, open market operations, quantitative tightening, forward guidance and forecasting.
  • Fiscal policy can complement monetary policy through taxation and government spending.
  • Exchange rates, supply shocks, demand changes, economic conditions and demographics help explain cross-country differences.

7. Lecture questions to practise

Why is it necessary to tackle inflation? A strong answer should cover consumer confidence and price stability, protecting purchasing power, and interest-rate stability.

Discuss taxation as a fiscal-policy tool against inflation. Explain how higher taxes can reduce disposable income and consumption, how supply-side tax incentives may improve productive capacity, and how budget surpluses can support fiscal discipline.

Week 6: Computing Unemployment Rates and Addressing Unemployment

Labour forceUnemployment rateLabour-force ratioEmployment policy

Learning objectives: Explain the labour force; interpret the labour-force–population ratio; compute the unemployment rate; and discuss techniques for addressing unemployment.

1. Why unemployment matters

Unemployment is an important economic indicator because it reflects the condition of a country's labour market. It affects individual livelihoods and has wider implications for economic well-being. A country's unemployment figures help show how effectively its available workforce is being used.

2. Meaning of the labour force

The labour force, also called the workforce, is the total number of people who are either employed or actively seeking employment in a particular country, region or economic sector. It is one of the factor inputs owned by households and is central to labour-market analysis.

Labour Force = Number of Employed Individuals + Number of Unemployed Individuals

The labour force is divided into two main groups:

  • Employed individuals: People who have jobs and are currently working, part-time or full-time, for pay or profit. The lecture also includes people temporarily absent from work because of illness, vacation or personal obligations.
  • Unemployed individuals: People without jobs who are available for work and actively seeking employment. Examples of active job search include submitting applications, attending interviews and registering with employment agencies.
Classification matters: A person without a job is not automatically counted as unemployed under the lecture's definition. They must also be available for work and actively seeking employment.

The lecture notes that retirees, students and people with disabilities may not be part of the labour force if they are not actively seeking employment. It also notes that discouraged workers who have stopped looking for work may not be included in official labour-force figures.

3. Labour-force–population ratio

This ratio expresses the labour force as a percentage of the working-age population. It helps assess the level of economic activity and the extent to which the available working-age population is employed or actively seeking work.

Labour-force–population ratio = (Labour Force ÷ Working-age Population) × 100

Interpretation: If the ratio is 60%, it means that 60% of the working-age population is in the labour force — either employed or actively seeking employment. It does not mean that 60% are employed, because unemployed job-seekers are also included in the labour force.

4. Computing the unemployment rate

The unemployment rate measures the percentage of the labour force that is unemployed. The denominator is the labour force, not the total population and not only the employed population.

Unemployment Rate = (Number of Unemployed ÷ Labour Force) × 100%

Worked example from the lecture

A country has a labour force of 150 million people, of whom 10 million are unemployed.

  1. Identify unemployed people = 10 million.
  2. Identify the labour force = 150 million.
  3. Divide: 10 ÷ 150 = 0.0667 (approximately).
  4. Convert to a percentage: 0.0667 × 100 = 6.67%.
Common mistake: Do not divide unemployed people by the total population when calculating the unemployment rate. Use the labour force as the denominator.

5. Techniques for addressing unemployment

The lecture emphasises a combination of short-term interventions and long-term strategies. Governments, businesses, educational institutions and communities all have roles to play.

  • Invest in education and skill development: Equip people with skills needed in changing industries. Cooperation between schools and industry can help make training relevant to labour-market demand.
  • Promote entrepreneurship: Support people who want to start businesses through resources, financial incentives and a favourable business environment. New businesses can create employment opportunities.
  • Labour-market flexibility: Enable businesses to adapt to changing economic conditions while balancing the needs of employers and employees.
  • Government policies and stimulus: Fiscal and monetary policies can stimulate economic activity. Infrastructure projects, tax incentives and targeted stimulus packages can create jobs and increase economic activity.
  • Workforce training and reskilling: Provide training that helps workers adapt to new industries and technologies and remain competitive.
  • Community-development initiatives: Design employment initiatives around the specific needs and challenges of local economies and regions.
  • Monitoring and evaluation: Regularly assess whether unemployment-reduction programmes are working. Use evidence to adjust policies as conditions change.
  • Social safety nets: Provide financial support, unemployment benefits, job-placement services and help for people returning to work after job loss.

6. Quick revision points

  • Labour force = employed + unemployed active job-seekers.
  • Unemployed people must be available for work and actively seeking it.
  • Unemployment rate uses the labour force as its denominator.
  • The labour-force–population ratio uses the working-age population as its denominator.
  • Education, entrepreneurship, flexibility, stimulus, reskilling, community initiatives, evaluation and safety nets are approaches identified in the lecture.

7. Worked interpretation practice

If a working-age population is 500,000 and the labour force is 300,000, the labour-force–population ratio is (300,000 ÷ 500,000) × 100 = 60%. This is an illustrative calculation applying the lecture's formula.

Week 7: Types of Taxation and Taxes Across Selected Countries

Regressive taxProgressive taxProportional taxTax revenue

Learning objectives: Explain regressive, progressive and proportional taxation; explain why tax income varies across countries; and analyse tax variations across countries.

1. Meaning of taxation and tax base

Taxation is the process of collecting money from individuals and businesses to finance government spending. Tax revenue supports public goods and services such as education, healthcare, infrastructure and national defence.

A tax base is the income on which a tax is imposed. The lecture stresses that government must understand what is being taxed in order to administer a tax appropriately. Countries differ in both the level of taxation and the types of taxes imposed.

2. The three main tax structures

Regressive tax

A regressive tax affects low-income earners more heavily than higher-income earners. The tax rate decreases as the tax base increases; there is an inverse relationship between the tax rate and the taxpayer's ability to pay. The lecture gives poll tax, lump-sum tax and Pigovian taxes as examples.

How to read the diagram (page 5): The plotted tax rate falls as the tax base/income rises. A lower-income taxpayer therefore faces a higher rate than a higher-income taxpayer in the regressive illustration.

Progressive tax

A progressive tax is structured according to taxpayers' ability to pay. The tax burden is lower for low-income earners and higher for richer taxpayers; the tax rate increases as income rises. The lecture describes income brackets or thresholds, where different rates apply to different income levels. Examples listed in the lecture include capital-gains tax, luxury sales tax and estate tax.

How to read the diagram (page 7): The tax-rate curve rises as the tax base increases, illustrating that a higher income level is associated with a higher tax rate.

Proportional tax

A proportional tax applies the same tax rate to all income groups. The amount paid in money can differ because incomes differ, but the percentage rate remains constant. The lecture notes that the burden may be heavier on poorer households because they tend to spend a higher fraction of income on consumables — described as a higher marginal propensity to consume.

How to read the diagram (page 9): The tax rate is shown as a horizontal line: the tax base changes, but the rate remains constant.

3. Forms of tax by tax base

  • Income tax: Tax on financial income earned by labour.
  • Corporate tax: Tax on financial income earned by business units.
  • Capital-gains tax: Tax imposed on profits from financial or physical assets.
  • Property tax: Tax imposed on the value of properties.
  • Inheritance tax: Tax imposed on bequeathed properties or valuables.
  • Sales tax: A consumption tax on the sale of goods and/or services, typically collected at the point of sale by the seller and passed to government.
  • Value-added tax (VAT): A tax on the value added to goods and services at each stage of production and distribution. The lecture notes that VAT is used in Europe and elsewhere, including Nigeria.

4. Why tax income varies across countries

Even countries with similar national income or tax bases may generate different tax income. The lecture identifies several reasons:

  • Tax base / national income level: The amount of income or activity available to be taxed influences potential revenue.
  • Efficiency of tax administration: The ability to assess, collect and manage taxes affects how much revenue is realised.
  • Tax evasion: Evasion reduces the revenue actually collected.
  • Judicial structure and penalties: The ability of the judiciary to penalise tax evaders can influence compliance.
  • Tax-compliance policies: Rules and administrative practices that support compliance affect collections.
  • Country-specific differences: The lecture notes that observed and unobserved differences between countries can produce different revenue outcomes even when national income levels are similar.

5. Comparing selected countries: what the lecture charts show

Graph A (pages 15–17): This chart presents tax income after deduction of product subsidies. The lecture observes Nigeria's blue line as the highest and the United Kingdom's orange line as the lowest among the displayed countries. It warns that this does not necessarily prove that Nigeria has the highest net tax performance or that the UK has the lowest tax income: differences in subsidy spending and the number of subsidised products are not fully revealed by the chart.

Graph B (pages 18–19): This chart compares tax revenue as a ratio of GDP for Nigeria, South Africa, the United States and the United Kingdom. The lecture describes Nigeria's tax-to-GDP ratio as declining from 2011 through 2020. The United States is described as relatively constant, with an increase in later years. The United Kingdom and South Africa are described as increasing over the period and having ratios higher than Nigeria and the United States.

How to interpret carefully: The lecture cautions that the charts do not reveal every factor behind tax revenue or subsidies. A chart can show a trend, but it may not be sufficient on its own to prove why the trend occurred.

6. Quick revision points

  • Regressive: rate falls as income rises; lower-income earners face the higher rate.
  • Progressive: rate rises as income rises; higher-income earners face the higher rate.
  • Proportional: the same percentage rate applies to every income group.
  • Tax income varies with the tax base, administration, evasion, judicial enforcement and compliance policies.
  • Tax-to-GDP compares tax revenue with the size of the economy.

Week 9: Money and Central Banks

Central bankingCBNMonetary policyMoney supply

Learning objectives: Explore the history and evolution of central banking; discuss the roles and functions of the Central Bank; and highlight the relationship between money and central banks.

1. History and evolution of central banking

The lecture traces the origin of central banking to the 17th century. Sveriges Riksbank in Sweden was established in 1668 and is described in the lecture as the world's first central bank. Other central banks later followed in England, France and other European countries.

Nigeria's central-banking history is linked to its colonial past. Before independence, the West African Currency Board served as the monetary authority. The need for a more centralised and independent monetary institution led to the establishment of the Central Bank of Nigeria.

2. Key milestones of the Central Bank of Nigeria

  • Establishment: The CBN was established in 1958 as Nigeria's central monetary authority.
  • Commencement of operations: It became fully operational on 1 July 1959 and assumed the functions of the West African Currency Board.
  • Currency issuance and control: It took control of currency issuance to support the stability and integrity of the Nigerian naira.
  • Monetary-policy implementation: It has played a role in formulating and implementing monetary policy to pursue economic stability.
  • Bank supervision and regulation: It supervises and regulates the Nigerian banking sector to support the soundness of financial institutions.
  • Foreign-exchange management: It manages foreign-exchange reserves and participates in the foreign-exchange market.
  • Adaptation to economic changes: Its policies have responded to challenges such as inflation, exchange-rate fluctuations and economic downturns.
  • Innovation and technology: The lecture notes the CBN's adoption of technological innovations, including digital-currency initiatives, to improve financial-system efficiency.

3. Roles and functions of a central bank

Central banks oversee a country's monetary system and implement monetary policy. The lecture describes them as the government's bank and the bankers' bank, with an important role in maintaining economic stability.

  • Monetary-policy formulation: Formulates and implements policy to pursue objectives such as price stability, full employment and economic growth. Tools mentioned include interest rates, open market operations and reserve requirements.
  • Currency issuance: Has the exclusive authority to issue and manage the national currency and ensure an adequate currency supply for the economy.
  • Banker to the government: Manages government accounts, handles transactions and facilitates government borrowing when necessary.
  • Banker's bank / lender of last resort: Provides emergency funding to financial institutions during crises, helping support banking-system stability.
  • Custodian of foreign-exchange reserves: Manages a country's foreign-exchange reserves and may participate in foreign-exchange markets to help stabilise the currency's value.
  • Regulation and supervision: Regulates and supervises financial institutions to protect the stability and integrity of the banking system.
  • Payment-systems oversight: Oversees payment systems so financial transactions can be secure and efficient.

4. Money and central banks

The lecture explains that the development of money and the central-banking system are connected. The money market — described in the lecture as the market for local currency — is primarily influenced by a cost: the interest rate.

A central bank is a financial institution with the privilege of controlling the production and distribution of money and credit in an economy. The lecture describes central banks as politically independent and non-market-based.

However, the central bank should not be confused with the money market itself. It is the monetary authority, but money-market outcomes depend on many market factors. The lecture distinguishes a central bank from other banks through its legal monopoly over issuing banknotes and its authority to regulate commercial banks.

Key distinction: A commercial bank provides banking services to households and businesses. A central bank is the monetary authority: it issues currency, formulates monetary policy and supervises the banking system.

5. Quick revision timeline

  • 1668: Sveriges Riksbank established in Sweden, identified by the lecture as the first central bank.
  • Before Nigerian independence: West African Currency Board served as the monetary authority.
  • 1958: Central Bank of Nigeria established.
  • 1 July 1959: CBN became fully operational and assumed the functions of the West African Currency Board.

6. Quick revision points

  • Central banks oversee the monetary system and implement monetary policy.
  • The CBN is Nigeria's central monetary authority.
  • Central banks issue currency, act as the government's bank and bankers' bank, manage reserves, supervise financial institutions and oversee payment systems.
  • The central bank is not the money market itself; market outcomes are influenced by wider market factors.
